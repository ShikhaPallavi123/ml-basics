Gradient Descent, Step by Step

By Shikha Pallavi

Part 3 showed that learning means making the cost smaller. Here is exactly how: look at the shape of the cost, feel the slope, and take small steps downhill. You will drag the numbers yourself.

cost curveslopesteplearning rate

One tiny dataset

Three points that sit exactly on a line: (1, 2), (2, 4), (3, 6). To keep things simple, fix θ0 = 0 so the model is just h(x) = θ1 · x. Only one number, θ1, is left to learn. Drag it and watch the line and the cost.

Cost: J(θ) = 1/(2m) · Σ(h(x) − y)² with m = 3. At θ1 = 1 the errors are 1, 2, 3, so J = (1 + 4 + 9) / 6 = 14/6 ≈ 2.33. At θ1 = 3 the errors are the same size, so J is again 2.33. At θ1 = 2 every error is 0 and J = 0.

A bowl, with one bottom

Plot the cost against θ1 and you get a smooth bowl. The very bottom, where J = 0, is the global minimum. For linear regression the bowl has no other dips to get stuck in, so going downhill always works.

The goal of training: find the θ1 at the bottom. Gradient descent gets there without trying every value.

Feel the slope

At any point on the bowl, the slope tells you which way is downhill.

Slope is negative

The curve is going down to the right. To go downhill, increase θ1.

Slope is positive

The curve is going up to the right. To go downhill, decrease θ1.

One rule covers both cases, the gradient descent update:

θ1 := θ1 − α · slope

The slope is the derivative of the cost, ∂J/∂θ1. Gradient means slope. Descent means moving downward. The Greek letter α is the learning rate, the size of each step. For this dataset the slope works out to 14/3 · (θ1 − 2).

Walk down the bowl

Pick a learning rate and a starting point, then press Step. The sign of the slope decides the direction. The learning rate decides how far you go.

 

The learning rate decides

α too small

Safe, but slow. It needs a great many steps to reach the bottom, which means slow training.

α too large

Each step overshoots the bottom. It can bounce around or diverge, getting further away and missing the minimum.

Try presets on the bowl above. Each button sets α and starts a fresh run.

 

For this dataset the update shrinks the distance to the minimum by the factor (1 − 14α/3) at every step. When that factor stays between −1 and 1, you converge. That happens for α below about 0.43. Past that, each step lands further away than the last one.

Quick check

What to remember

Cost curvePlot J against the parameter. For linear regression it is a bowl with one bottom.
SlopeNegative slope: increase the parameter. Positive slope: decrease it.
Updateθ := θ − α · slope, repeated until the cost stops shrinking.
Learning rateToo small is slow. Too large overshoots and can diverge.
