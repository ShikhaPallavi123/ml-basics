How does a computer learn without being told the rules?

By Shikha Pallavi

Your inbox knows what is spam. Nobody sat down and wrote a rule for every sneaky subject line ever invented. So how does it know? In the next few minutes you will play with the answer, not just read it.

spamgroupsrewardslearning

Be the rule writer

Imagine your job is to catch spam by writing rules. Pick one word and say, “any email with this word is spam.” Next to your rule is a tiny program that learned from 12 example emails. Try to beat it.

Incoming emailMy ruleLearner
Try the words free, cash or meeting. Which one catches the most spam without false alarms?

Notice what happens when spammers write fr33 instead of free. Your rule stops working, and you would have to write a new one, again and again. The learner has the same weakness until it sees a few new examples. Then it fixes itself with no rule-writing at all. That is the whole idea of machine learning.

How the learner works: for every word it counts how often it appeared in spam emails and in normal ones. Words that mostly show up in spam get a positive score, words that mostly show up in normal mail get a negative score, and the scores of an email’s words are added up. It is a small counting trick, not a magic brain.

Three ways to learn

The spam learner had an answer key: each example came marked spam or not. That is only one way a computer can learn. Here are all three.

1 · With an answer key

Every example comes with the right answer. The computer studies the pairs and learns to answer new ones. This is called supervised learning, and it covers two kinds of jobs. Tap each task to see which kind it is.

Is the answer a label (a category) or a number?
2 · With no answer key

Here nobody says what anything is. Below are 42 dots. Nobody told the computer there are three clumps. Press the button and watch it work them out by shuffling guesses until they stop changing.

 

This is called unsupervised learning. The method here repeats two moves: give every dot to its nearest centre, then move each centre to the middle of its dots.

3 · By trial and reward

A tiny robot starts at the bottom left. The gold star is a treat (+10). The red squares are traps (−10). Every step costs −1, so wandering is a bad idea. It begins knowing nothing. Slide to give it more practice runs and see what it figures out.

 

This is reinforcement learning. The robot keeps a score for every move from every square and nudges those scores after each run. The path you see is simply “pick the highest-scoring move at each square.”

What to remember

The big ideaDon’t write the rules. Show examples and let the computer find them.
Answer keyExamples come with answers. The job is a label (spam or not) or a number (a price).
No answer keyThe computer finds natural groups by itself.
RewardsIt tries things and keeps whatever earns the most.
